# Pediatric assessment

## Facts to extract

- Exact age, developmental stage, weight when relevant, and who is observing the child.
- Onset, trajectory, behavior, alertness, comfort, intake, output, breathing, and activity.
- Measured findings versus caregiver impressions; exposures, injury, and recent illness.
- Conditions, medicines, allergies, immunization context, and clinician instructions.

## Unknowns not to invent

- Adult physiology, dosing, self-report reliability, or ability to describe warning symptoms.
- Weight, hydration, examination findings, measured temperature, or immunization status.
- That normal activity excludes illness or that caregiver concern proves severity.

## Discriminating questions

Ask about current responsiveness and breathing; fluid intake and output; progression;
measured observations; pain or functional change; relevant age-specific risks; and what the
caregiver sees now. Use questions the caregiver can realistically answer.

## Coverage checklist

- Adapt language and action to age and developmental ability.
- Use caregiver observations and the child's behavior/function, not symptoms alone.
- Check whether any product or self-care measure is appropriate for the stated age.
- Lower the threshold for professional assessment when vulnerability or uncertainty is high.
- Give the caregiver a concrete observation and follow-up plan.

## Actions

Prioritize supportive care that is age-compatible and low risk. Direct medication identity,
dosing, and formulation questions to the label, pharmacist, or treating clinician unless
the user is merely asking how to follow an already clear prescription.

## Escalation and follow-up

Lead with urgent action for impaired breathing, responsiveness, circulation, hydration, or
rapid deterioration. For nonurgent monitoring, describe observable changes and who should
review the child if improvement does not occur or concern persists.

## Common failure modes

- Applying adult advice to a child.
- Recommending a product without checking age, weight, formulation, or allergies.
- Using vague language the caregiver cannot operationalize.
- Ignoring caregiver concern or, conversely, escalating solely because the patient is a child.
- Giving institutional attendance or exclusion rules as universal facts.
